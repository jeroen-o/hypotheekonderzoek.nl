ESG data for your mortgage portfolio

A practical guide for risk, finance and sustainability teams at mortgage lenders. Which ESG data do supervisors and European rules require, and how do you compare your portfolio with the market?

1. Summary

For most Dutch lenders, mortgages are the largest asset class. As a result, the collateral, the home, largely determines the ESG profile of the balance sheet. European and Dutch rules increasingly require that profile to be measured, managed and reported on.

  • Environmental: energy efficiency and climate risk of the collateral. The energy label is the most important data point for this, and at the same time an imperfect one.
  • Social: access to credit for first-time buyers, single-person households, older borrowers and other groups, and preventing bias in underwriting and models.
  • Governance: traceable data, documented definitions and demonstrable monitoring.

Your own portfolio data tells you where you stand. Only a comparison with the market tells you whether that is good or bad. This whitepaper sets out which data matter and how to use market reference data.

2. Why mortgage ESG data matters now

Three developments are converging:

  1. Prudential supervision. Since 30 June 2021, the EBA Guidelines on loan origination and monitoring have required institutions to incorporate ESG factors into their risk appetite, their credit policy and the valuation of collateral.
  2. Disclosure. Large and listed institutions report ESG templates every six months, including loans collateralised by immovable property by energy efficiency.
  3. National data collection. Under the Wet rapportage hypotheekmarkt DNB (Dutch Mortgage Market Reporting Act), banks, insurers, pension funds and investment institutions will report loan-level data to DNB, including the energy label.

In addition, the energy label is visibly linked to credit risk. Kadaster (Netherlands Land Registry) reports that in the second half of 2025 the average loan-to-value for homes with label A or better was 85,1%, compared with 89,5% for label D or worse.

4. E: energy labels and climate risk

The energy label: indispensable, but imperfect

The energy label is the most widely used indicator of residential energy efficiency, and the only one available at scale at loan level. It does have limitations:

  • A label is not consumption. Research by CBS (Statistics Netherlands) with PCAF (2020) showed that actual energy consumption can vary widely within a single label class. CO₂ estimates based on labels are therefore always indicative.
  • Missing and outdated labels. Not every home has a registered label, and a label may predate a renovation.
  • Point-in-time snapshot. The label at origination says nothing about subsequent improvements. Recording whether a customer finances energy-saving measures makes the trajectory visible.

What you need to know as a minimum

  • The distribution of your new lending by label class, and the quarterly trend
  • The share of label A or better compared with the market
  • How often energy-saving measures are included in the financing, by label group
  • The relationship between label and risk, such as LTV and loan-to-income by label group
  • Indicative financed CO₂ emissions using the PCAF methodology

Physical climate risk

Template D 05.00 asks for exposure to physical climate risks. For residential mortgages in the Netherlands, these are mainly flooding, waterlogging and drought-related foundation problems. To assess this, you link the location of the collateral to external risk maps. The regional distribution of your inflow is a first, simple step.

5. S: accessible and fair lending

The S in ESG often receives less attention for mortgages, but it is relevant to both supervision and reputation.

  • Accessibility. Do you reach first-time buyers, single-person households, older borrowers, the self-employed and flexible workers as well as the market does? A structurally lower share may point to underwriting that is too strict or unclear.
  • Vulnerable groups. The CPB (Netherlands Bureau for Economic Policy Analysis, 2023) concludes that homeowners have on average become more resilient, but that first-time buyers, young people and those with lower incomes or limited assets remain vulnerable.
  • Bias. The EBA requires institutions that use automated models to detect and prevent bias. A difference in conversion between, for example, men and women, or between age groups, is not proof of discrimination. It is, however, a signal to review underwriting criteria and models.
  • Interest-only mortgages. According to the AFM, more than half of mortgage debt is interest-only and many of these mortgages mature between 2035 and 2040. The AFM expects lenders to engage at-risk customers and to keep monitoring them.

6. G: data quality, traceability and governance

The EBA requires a data infrastructure granular enough to track credit risk at loan level, including the criteria applied at origination (§ 60–61). For ESG data, this means:

  • documented definitions, for example which label source, which reference date and how missing labels are treated;
  • version control and an audit trail for reported figures;
  • periodic data quality checks, covering completeness, plausibility and consistency between reports;
  • a designated owner for each metric, and reporting to the management board.

7. Market reference data alongside your own portfolio

Your own data answers the question of how green or how accessible your portfolio is. Whether that is good can only be answered with a benchmark. Market reference data helps in four ways:

  1. Interpretation. Is 50% label A or better in your new lending high or low? That depends on what is being financed in the market.
  2. Target setting. For sustainable lending, the EBA asks for qualitative and, where relevant, quantitative targets (§ 59). A market reference makes a target realistic and testable.
  3. Early warning. Tracking calculations and applications in the advice process reveals shifts earlier than your own portfolio does.
  4. Accountability. An independent reference strengthens your reporting to the board, auditors and supervisors.

8. Checklist for your organisation

  1. Do we record the energy label, with source and date, for every new loan?
  2. Do we know the share of missing labels, and how we treat them?
  3. Do we report the label distribution of new lending to the board every quarter?
  4. Do we compare that distribution with the market?
  5. Do we record whether the customer finances energy-saving measures as part of the loan?
  6. Do we know our portfolio's exposure to flooding and foundation risk?
  7. Do we monitor accessibility for first-time buyers, single-person households and older borrowers against the market?
  8. Do we periodically test underwriting and models for bias?
  9. Do we have sustainable lending targets, and do we track them?
  10. Are our ESG definitions and sources documented and traceable?
